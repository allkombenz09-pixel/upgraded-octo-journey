### Часть 1 — ❌ неверно → исправлено
> And it all started with a boy... from a tiny farm in southern Sweden.

Elmtaryd was not tiny. It was a 449-hectare (about 1,110 acres) property, largely forest, bought by Ingvar's German-born grandparents Achim and Franziska Kamprad in the 1890s. The IKEA Museum gives the 449 ha size, and Wikipedia calls it the largest farm in the area. Some secondary sources (e.g. sweden.se) call it 'a small farm', but that conflicts with the museum's own figure. The family was cash-strapped, but it was not a smallholding. Ingvar was also not born there: he was born on 30 March 1926 in Pjätteryd, on his mother's family farm Majtorp, and moved to Elmtaryd the year he turned seven (1933).

A five-year-old in Småland did not buy anything in Stockholm himself. IKEA's history page says he 'enlisted his aunt in Stockholm to buy big, less expensive cartons and send them to him'. In Kamprad's own account (the 1998 book with Bertil Torekull, read via its German edition), his aunt bought his first 100 boxes for 88 öre in total at Stockholm's '88-öre bazaar' (today Buttericks) and didn't even charge him postage. 'Cheaper than anyone else' is not supported by any source; the standard wording is 'at a low price'. ### Часть 2 — ⚠️ неточно → исправлено
> It sounds obvious today. But back then, it changed everything.

The IKEA Museum says IKEA did not invent flatpacks. Thonet chairs were shipped flat in the 1800s, and Sweden had a flatpack furniture series, Triva, in 1944. LÖVET was not even IKEA's first self-assembly product: the DELFI, KÖKSA and RIGA tables were in the 1953 catalogue. Kamprad said IKEA was not first, but was 'the first systematically to develop the idea commercially'. Britannica calls flat-packing the company's breakthrough. Lundgren's real contribution was popularising flat-pack across the whole range.

### Часть 3 — ⚠️ неточно → исправлено
> But success makes enemies. Swedish furniture retailers are furious about IKEA's low prices. They pressure suppliers to stop working with Ingvar.

The content is right but the timing is wrong. Placed after the 1958 store, it suggests the hostility began after the store opened. It started much earlier. In 1950, other exhibitors objected to IKEA showing consumer prices at the S:t Eriksmässan fair in Stockholm, and that December the fair told Kamprad he would not be admitted to future events. By 1952, according to the IKEA Museum, the Swedish furniture retailers' association was threatening suppliers: 'sell to IKEA and we'll stop buying from you.' The boycott peaked in the mid-to-late 1950s. Kamprad worked around it with suppliers selling under other names or shipping to neutral addresses, by rebuying through a separate company (Ikano), and by designing IKEA's own furniture.
